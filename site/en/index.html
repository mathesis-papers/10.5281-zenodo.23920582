MathOverflow 512579, revision 1: The elementary symmetric inequality for a Hermitian Jordan product

Haobo Ma, Wenlin Zhang

Paper

Scalar inequalities often compare twice a product with a sum of squares, but matrices introduce an obstacle: multiplication depends on order. Haobo Ma and Wenlin Zhang show that every sum of products of a fixed number of eigenvalues of the symmetric mixed product is bounded, even in absolute value, by its counterpart for the sum of squares; the original matrices may even have negative eigenvalues.

Transposing a complex matrix and conjugating its entries leaves a Hermitian matrix unchanged; positive semidefinite ones have nonnegative eigenvalues. Malkoun’s question concerns such matrices X,Y, comparing XY+YX with X²+Y². The former is the Jordan product, here without a factor of one half. For an eigenvalue list, the kth elementary symmetric function sums all products from k distinct positions, respecting multiplicities. The first is the trace, the last the determinant. Joseph Malkoun asked whether each quantity for the mixed product is at most its counterpart for the sum of squares: a scalar comparison extended to an entire family of spectral summaries.

The written theorem allows arbitrary complex matrices A,B with p rows and n columns. With the star denoting conjugate transpose, put H=A*B+B*A and R=A*A+B*B. A principal submatrix retains the same selected rows and columns. The authors bound the absolute determinant of every principal submatrix of H by the determinant of its counterpart in R. Summing bounds the absolute value of each elementary symmetric function of H by its counterpart for R, from index zero through n, with the zeroth function defined as one. Setting A=X and B=Y proves the stronger statement for arbitrary Hermitian matrices, including those with negative eigenvalues.

Negative values are genuinely relevant. In the paper’s example, X has rows (1,0),(0,0), and Y has rows (1,1),(1,1). Both are positive semidefinite, but XY+YX has rows (2,1),(1,0) and determinant −1; X²+Y² has determinant 2. The mixed product is therefore not positive semidefinite, yet its absolute determinant is bounded. The argument cannot simply assume both comparison matrices have nonnegative spectra.

For a chosen column set, stack the columns of A above those of B, then make a second stack by swapping the blocks. A Gram matrix records inner products between columns. Both stacks have the same Gram matrix, the corresponding principal submatrix of R, while their cross-inner-products give that of H. The determinant form of Cauchy–Schwarz bounds the squared absolute cross determinant by the product of the two Gram determinants. The authors derive it using Cauchy–Binet, which expresses these determinants through lists of row minors: the cross determinant becomes their inner product, and the Gram determinants become their squared lengths. Ordinary Cauchy–Schwarz now applies. Taking square roots gives the principal bound because the Gram determinant is nonnegative. Finally, elementary symmetric functions equal sums of same-size principal determinants, so the triangle inequality completes the proof. No inverse is needed, so singular cases are included. The authors credit related compound-matrix methods to J. K. Merikoski and A. Virtanen; the useful step here makes both Gram matrices identical.

The elementary symmetric functions are the coefficients of the determinant polynomial. For any complex number z, with |z| its magnitude and I the identity matrix, their bounds give |det(I+zH)| ≤ det(I+|z|R). Separately, for three pairwise commuting n-by-n positive semidefinite matrices A,B,C, set S=A+B+C, Q=A²+B²+C², and F=6(A³+B³+C³)+S³. The authors prove det F ≥ 5ⁿ det S det Q and related eigenvalue-product comparisons. A common orthonormal coordinate system makes all three matrices diagonal, reducing the argument to three nonnegative numbers: six times their sum of cubes plus the cube of their sum is at least five times their sum times their sum of squares. Decomposing the difference into nonnegative terms establishes a coordinatewise comparison, which products and sums preserve.

The note reports a Lean proof only of Malkoun’s original positive-semidefinite assertion, in positive dimension and for indices one through n. The stronger extensions and additional corollaries have written proofs, not that stated Lean coverage. The reported computations use Gaussian integers, complex numbers with integer real and imaginary parts, to check finite instances of identities and bounds exactly; they do not prove the all-dimensions assertions. This settles the first question, not a universal rule for promoting scalar inequalities to noncommutative ones. Noncommuting three-matrix conjectures and a proposed rank-one reduction remain untreated here. The note also credits cubesteak’s earlier posted proof of a normalized three-matrix determinant inequality. Its particular contribution is a mechanism controlling every principal determinant, without separate eigenvalue-by-eigenvalue comparisons.
